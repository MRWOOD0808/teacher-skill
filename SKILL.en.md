# 教员skill: Investigation–Contradiction–Practice Method

> English backup. The canonical executable entrypoint is the Chinese [SKILL.md](SKILL.md). The user-facing name remains **教员skill**.

## Objective

Turn a complex, changing, and partially observed problem into **verifiable factual judgments, a primary constraint, a stage-specific focus, a bounded action plan, and a learning loop**. The method is based on the studied text of *Selected Works of Mao Zedong*, but it is designed for modern tasks. Do not transplant historical conclusions, political identity categories, or methods of warfare.

## When to use

Use when a problem involves multiple stakeholders, conflicting interests, unclear causality, limited resources, or changing conditions—for example, stalled product growth, failed organizational coordination, delayed projects, community-governance questions, research-direction choices, or strategic tradeoffs.

Do not use for:

- low-complexity lookup, translation, or formatting tasks;
- impersonating Mao Zedong, speaking for the author, or proving a predetermined political conclusion;
- labeling groups as enemies and using that label to discriminate, manipulate, surveil, coerce, or harm;
- unlawful, violent, harassing, deceptive-propaganda, or dissent-suppressing action;
- replacing professional medical, legal, financial, safety, or engineering standards.

If there is an urgent risk to people, compliance, or production systems, first take the minimum necessary, reversible action within existing authority to limit harm and preserve evidence. Do not delay safety action merely because the investigation is incomplete.

## Inputs

Obtain when possible:

- the goal, current state, and observable gap;
- time horizon, affected parties, constraints, and non-negotiable boundaries;
- known facts, data provenance, historical changes, prior attempts, and results;
- each stakeholder’s interests, capabilities, position, and observable behavior;
- available resources, decision authority, deadline, and success metrics.

When information is insufficient, do not guess. Produce a **provisional problem statement, critical unknowns, and minimum investigation plan**. Ask the user only when missing information would materially change the authorization or objective.

## Operating principles

1. **Investigation precedes conclusion.** Separate facts, interpretations, hypotheses, and value judgments. Books, authority, and precedent remain subject to field evidence.
2. **Analyze the concrete case concretely.** No template can replace analysis of the object, stage, conditions, and relationships.
3. **Find the primary contradiction and then its principal aspect.** Here, the “primary contradiction” means the relationship currently constraining the goal most strongly; its “principal aspect” is the factor temporarily dominating that relationship. Both can change with the stage.
4. **Hold the whole and the focus together.** Do not spread effort evenly or optimize a local metric in isolation. Organize supporting work around one central task.
5. **Test general proposals in concrete pilots.** Form hypotheses from cases and test them across different cases. Never present one pilot as a universal law.
6. **Close the feedback loop.** Gather frontline views, synthesize them into a plan, return the plan to practice, and revise it from results.
7. **Use collective judgment and named accountability.** Allow substantive dissent on major issues, form a clear decision, then assign one accountable owner, checkpoints, and a review date.
8. **Constrain modern transfer by rights and evidence.** Replace friend–enemy or class labels with stakeholders, enabling conditions, resistance, and intermediate states. Use only lawful, voluntary, nonviolent, and auditable action.

## Workflow

### 1. Rewrite the task as an investigable question

Use one sentence:

> Within [scope/stage], there is an [observable gap] between [goal] and [current state]. We must determine [key cause or tradeoff] under [constraints].

List success metrics and stop conditions. If the goal is unmeasurable, internally conflicting, or outside the user’s authority, surface that conflict first.

### 2. Build an evidence ledger and investigate

For each item, record source, time, sample, observable fact, interpretation, reliability, counterevidence, and gap. Cover present and historical data, positive and negative cases, management and frontline views, aggregates and representative cases. Do not treat one interview or one metric as the whole.

Aim the investigation at decision-changing unknowns. Prefer primary records, direct observation, and user or frontline feedback; use secondary sources as support. For detailed designs, read [method-cards.en.md](references/method-cards.en.md).

### 3. Draw a contradiction–stage map

List the main relationships. For each, record the two parties or requirements, evidence on both sides, interdependence, conflict symptoms, changing strength, current stage, and intervention points. Then answer:

- Which relationship most constrains the current objective, and what evidence supports that ranking?
- Which factor currently dominates, and what signal would reverse the ranking?
- Is this a structural contradiction, a stage-specific bottleneck, or a surface symptom?
- Have we ignored the other side, the future, the whole, the difficulty, or a counterexample?

“Primary” does not mean “only,” and a contradiction does not imply eliminating one side.

### 4. Analyze stakeholders and conditions

Classify by observable interests, capabilities, behavior, and change conditions: active support, persuadable support, neutral or waiting, explicit obstruction, and affected but absent. Explain how to protect rights, obtain candid feedback, enlarge lawful common interests, and reduce avoidable resistance.

Every classification must remain revisable. Never permanently classify a person from identity, group membership, or a single statement.

### 5. Select the stage focus and action sequence

Choose one objective that can materially change the whole situation. Concentrate limited resources there while preserving the support required to keep the system functioning. State:

- what to do now, what to defer, and what not to do;
- why this is the present leverage point;
- the pilot, scale conditions, and exit conditions;
- optimistic, base, and deteriorating scenarios with observable triggers.

In modern use, “concentrating strength” means resource prioritization and sequenced problem solving only; it never means attacking people.

### 6. Organize execution

Translate the general objective into concrete guidance for one or two representative units or settings. Name the accountable owner, participants, authority, deliverables, numerical measures, information cadence, and review date. Preserve dissent records for major decisions. Circulate agendas and material before meetings; after a clear decision, execute through named owners.

### 7. Revise understanding from practical results

Before acting, write expected outcomes, leading indicators, failure signals, and counterevidence. After acting, compare expected with actual:

- Which judgment was supported, weakened, or overturned?
- Did the principal relationship or stage change?
- Which outcomes plausibly came from the intervention, and which from external change?
- Should the next cycle scale, adjust, pause, or exit?

Treat failure as evidence for updating the model; do not explain away counterexamples with slogans.

## Output format

1. **Conclusion and confidence** — best current judgment, explicitly separating fact, inference, and unknown.
2. **Problem and stage** — goal gap, boundary, and current stage.
3. **Evidence-ledger summary** — supporting evidence, counterevidence, source quality, and critical gaps.
4. **Contradiction–stakeholder map** — primary contradiction, principal aspect, other important relationships, and change signals.
5. **Stage focus** — do / defer / do not do, with reasons.
6. **Action table** — action, owner, time, resources, metric, trigger, and exit condition.
7. **Practice loop** — review cadence, feedback channels, and next revision point.
8. **Boundaries** — risk, ethics, professional standards, and authority limits.

When the user requests sources, cite volume, essay title, and a searchable phrase. Distinguish **the author’s historical claim, synthesis grounded in the text, and modern transfer design**. Read [sources.en.md](references/sources.en.md); do not invent page numbers.

## Pre-delivery checks

- Did the conclusion follow the evidence and remain falsifiable?
- Did the analysis include history/present, whole/part, support/opposition, and positive/counterexample views?
- Is the primary contradiction ranked with evidence and switch signals?
- Did the plan avoid equal effort everywhere, uncontrolled expansion, and goals without methods?
- Are ownership, numbers, time, feedback, and exit conditions explicit?
- Did the output avoid transplanting historical political or military methods into modern organizations?
- Are version, evidence, professional, and authority limits explicit?

For the executed normal, insufficient-information, inapplicable, and emergency-boundary tests, read [validation.en.md](references/validation.en.md).
